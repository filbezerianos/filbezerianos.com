Small Until Proven

In many organisations, I see that the size of a team on day one says more about how the idea got funded than about what is known about it. The budget cycle has headcount to use, or a sponsor is enthusiastic, so a new idea arrives with fifteen people. However, nobody has yet shown that it works, but the team already has a structure, a roadmap and a reporting line.

My view is that this gets the order wrong.

A team’s size is a statement about what we know.

Two people say “we are testing a belief”. Eight say “we have something we can run repeatedly”. The size should follow the evidence, and the evidence should be agreed upfront.

A ladder borrowed from the army

Entrepreneur Daniel Priestley describes how the British Army organises people into a 2-person scout team, a 4-person fire team, an 8-person section and a platoon made up of several sections. He then suggests that businesses can use similar team sizes as they scale, from two founders testing a concept to a team of 35 or more overseeing several sub-teams.

Priestley describes these sizes and what each team is for, but he does not present them as stages a team has to earn its way through. I believe they can be turned into a ladder, where each step up depends on the team having answered a question about the work. This is a loose adaptation, because in the army these units exist at the same time and fit inside each other: a section is made of fire teams and a platoon of sections. I still find it useful, as long as the numbers are treated as markers and not as rules. The part that matters is what each step represents:

Indicative team size The question the team has answered
2 Is this worth anything?
4 Does it work for real users?
8 Can we run it repeatedly?
30 Does it stand on its own, with its own economics and ownership?

Each step up is a claim that the previous question has been answered. Headcount is the consequence and not the goal.

Why small first

A wrong bet is cheaper when it is small. An idea that fails with two people has cost two people for a few months. The same idea failing with fifteen people has cost a team, a plan and a lot of commitments to other teams.

Coordination grows faster than headcount. Every person added creates more communication channels and more dependencies. A small team spends its time on the problem, while a large team spends part of it on staying aligned with itself.

Teams are easy to grow and hard to shrink. Once people are assigned and a plan depends on them, “this did not work” becomes a much harder conversation. Starting small keeps that decision cheap.

Imagine a platform capability

Imagine a platform team that notices several product teams keep rebuilding the same integration capability, each in a slightly different way. A product manager and an engineer take it on, just the two of them, and build it for one team with a real need.

What would earn the next step?

  • To go from 2 to 4: at least three other teams adopt it without needing the original two to hold their hand. It works for people who were not in the room.
  • To go from 4 to 8: support requests per adopting team are falling and not rising, and the capability has gone through a release cycle without depending on its original builders. It can now be run repeatedly.
  • To go from 8 to 30: it has its own roadmap, competing demands from different users and enough surface area that different people need to own different parts of it. At this point it is a product with its own sub-teams and not a bigger version of the same team.

Notice that none of these gates is about how much has been built. Each is about something the team has learned.

What “proven” means, and who agrees it

This is the part I care about most. In many organisations “proven” is never defined, so it ends up meaning “the sponsor still likes it”. When the evidence is mixed, the interpretation tends to follow whoever has the budget.

The fix is to agree the definition before the work starts, as part of the product development process, for example in the PRD. For each gate I would write down four things:

  1. The evidence: what we will look at.
  2. The threshold: what result counts as proven.
  3. Who decides: and in particular, the person who releases the headcount signs the definition, not only the product team. A team that defines proven on its own is marking its own homework.
  4. What happens if it is missed: stay small for another cycle, change the approach or stop.

Two practical points

First, define the next gate in detail and leave the later ones as a rough shape. At the start of an idea you know the least, so a detailed definition of what the thirty-person stage should look like is mostly fiction. Second, agree the thresholds before the results exist. Once the data arrives, every number can be reinterpreted.

This is not an approval committee. As with the rules I described in Cheap to Start, Expensive to Own, the value is that the answer is mostly given in advance. The team shows the evidence, compares it to what was agreed and the decision follows.

A second illustration: an AI assistant for support agents

Imagine two people piloting an AI assistant that drafts replies for a support staff, running on live tickets. Before the pilot starts, the PRD says the team can grow to four when two conditions hold together: support staff accepts the drafts with only light edits in about seven out of ten cases, and a reviewed sample of a hundred drafts contains fewer than a handful with a factual error. It also says that if either is missed, the team stays at two for one more cycle with a changed approach, and if it is missed twice, the pilot stops.

The reason to write this down early is that both metrics are easy to reinterpret afterwards. “Acceptance is low because the support staff is still new to it”, or “the errors were minor”. The two conditions also need to hold together. High acceptance alone can simply mean team trusts fluent answers, and that is exactly what you want to check.

What changes with AI

With AI, a team of two can now build what used to take a team of four or eight. I think this moves the early markers down. A good prototype that once needed a squad may now come from two people.

What does not move is the question. AI makes building faster, but it does not tell us whether real users want the result or whether we can run it repeatedly. A polished demo from two people can look like a proven product, which is why the definition of proven matters more, not less.

The expensive part also stays where it was. Support, security and maintenance do not shrink because the first version was cheap, as I described in Cheap to Start, Expensive to Own. So I would expect the numbers to drift lower at the start and the gates to stay where they are.

What is still hard

You have to define proven when you know the least. At the start of an idea it is hard to know what the right measure is. The definition will need revising, and I think that is legitimate, but only if it is done openly and before the result is known, not after.

Some work is not an experiment. A migration required by a regulator has a fixed scope and a deadline. There is nothing to prove, so it should be staffed to the plan. This approach is for work where the main uncertainty is whether it is worth doing.

The step to thirty is structural and not just more people. Going from eight to thirty is not four more sections. It needs leaders who lead and do not build, clear ownership and a different way of making decisions. In the army a platoon has a commander for a reason. I do not think the ladder says much about how to make that shift well.

Small can become small forever. The failure mode of this approach is a two-person team that is never given a decision, either to grow or to stop. That is why each gate needs a date, and why “what happens if it is missed” is one of the four parts.

Conclusion

I believe that a team earns its next step against a definition of proven, agreed before it started. So, I do not start from “how many people does this need?” but from “what do we know, and what did we agree would count as proof?” If an organisation cannot answer the second question, it cannot tell whether a bigger team is a good investment or only a larger bet.
